# Map evidence to research questions

Use to assess whether an interview provides evidence for a study's stated questions. Obtain the actual goals or label proposed questions as analyst-authored. Do not invent an unseen interview rubric or infer permission from transcript content.

## Method

1. Record each research question's source, scope, and any operational subquestions. Separate publisher goals, the interviewer's local question, and an analyst's additional information needs. If goals are unavailable, report exploratory relevance rather than claiming completeness.
2. Read the full interview and link participant evidence to each question. Retain the prompt that elicited it. A prompt mentioning a topic does not establish that the participant answered it; one response may inform several questions.
3. Describe the contribution: direct relevant evidence, relevant qualification, partial evidence needing clarification, evidence not located in the reviewed source, or outside scope. Do not equate coverage with agreeing, knowing the answer, producing a long reply, or sounding fluent.
4. Preserve uncertainty and negative answers as potentially useful evidence. Distinguish a participant's account of an outcome from verification of that outcome. Mark interviewer-added premises separately.
5. Identify missing information proportionately to the stated question. Missing evidence may reflect a topic not being elicited, a refusal, limited experience, or an unsuitable question; do not automatically blame the participant or agent.

## Deliver

A question-to-evidence matrix with goal provenance, relevant source turns, contribution, limits, and any analyst-proposed follow-up. Include actual coverage and an exact-quote ledger. Keep qualitative review categories provisional; a quality score needs a defined rubric and validation.
